In the APSA Public Scholarship Program, graduate students in political science produce summaries of new research in the American Political Science Review. This piece, written by Raymundo Lopez, covers the new article by Agustin Markarian, Jacob S. Hacker, Mackenzie Lockhart, and Zoltan Hajnal, “Race, Responsiveness, and Representation in U.S. Lawmaking”.

Power By the People
The ancient Greek word dēmokratia, meaning “rule by the people,” has shaped democratic practices for more than two millennia. Derived from the root words dēmos (“people”) and kratos (“power”), this simple word continues to define how we understand modern democracies, including the United States.

For much of America’s history, political barriers like slavery and discriminatory voting laws prevented racial and ethnic minority communities from exercising full political power. The civil rights movement of the 1960s marked a turning point, expanding access to the ballot for Black Americans and opening the door for Latinos and Asian Americans. In the decades since, the number of elected officials of color has grown significantly. So, if government is truly “ruled by the people,” then it should respond to the preferences of all its citizens—but does it?

All Roads Lead to Parties
Despite the historic gains racial minority groups have made, they continue to lag behind white Americans in wealth and other resources that shape political influence. This raises a fundamental democratic question. If democracy truly means “rule by the people,” why doesn’t greater political inclusion also drive equal policy responsiveness for all people?

To better understand whether federal lawmaking responds equally to Americans of different racial backgrounds, Markarian, Hacker, Lockhart, and Hajnal argue that we must pay closer attention to partisan control in the federal government. Political parties, they argue, have become the primary engines of representation in American politics, with voters of color aligning more closely with the Democratic Party and white voters leaning Republican. One might therefore expect Democratic governments to be more responsive to communities of color, yet competing studies suggest this is not always the case. Exactly who is behind the wheel of representation in Washington remains an open question. This leads the authors to two central questions: First, is national policy more responsive to the preferences of white Americans than to those of people of color? Second, under what conditions do these disparities widen or narrow?

It All Depends on Who’s in Charge
To resolve these questions, the political scientists gather data on major federal policy issues and match them with information from the largest politics-focused survey in the United States. This approach allows the team to compare what actually became law (or failed to become law) in Congress against the actual preferences of more than 500,000 Black, Latino, Asian and white Americans over a period spanning two decades. Put simply, if a respondent supported a policy that later became law, or opposed one that failed, their preference “won.” If the opposite occurred, it “lost.”

“With the Supreme Court narrowing portions of the Voting Rights Act and states continuing to redraw electoral maps through partisan gerrymandering, questions about equal representation remain as urgent as ever.”What do they uncover? Across 4 broad areas (economic, cultural, defense policy, and government operations), they find no significant racial gaps in representation. Judged only by these results, American democracy appears to respond equally across racial groups. But once the researchers account for who controls government, a very different picture emerges.

Under Democratic presidents and Democratic-controlled Senates, racial disparities in policy responsiveness shrink substantially and, in some cases, even reverse. Under Republican control, however, those disparities widen considerably. White Americans become significantly more likely to see their preferred policies enacted, while Latinos and Asian Americans become less likely to see their preferences translated into law. Black Americans experience the largest decline in policy responsiveness, losing 7 to 9 percentage points more often than white Americans.

The authors also make a surprising observation: Black Americans don’t receive stronger representation in states where they make up a larger share of the population. Among the explanations they consider, white racial resentment emerges as the strongest. This widely used political science measure captures the degree to which white Americans feel that racial progress has come at their expense. States with larger Black populations tend to exhibit higher levels of white racial resentment, a pattern the authors associate with Republican senators being more likely to favor the preferences of white constituents over Black constituents. Once the authors account for white racial resentment, the unexpected relationship between Black population size and representation largely disappears.

Everybody Wants to Rule the World
Markarian and colleagues are careful to acknowledge the limits of their data and the complexity of what they find. That said, their novel approach to a longstanding question allows them to examine how policy responsiveness varies across issues and over time. In doing so, the authors show us that democratic representation is not simply about who can vote; it is about who calls the plays in Washington. This helps explain why partisan control can shape whose voices the government responds to.

As Markarian and colleagues suggest, access to the ballot box does not automatically translate into equal influence over the laws that govern daily life. This lesson feels especially timely today. With the Supreme Court narrowing portions of the Voting Rights Act and states continuing to redraw electoral maps through partisan gerrymandering, questions about equal representation remain as urgent as ever.

In the end, democracy’s oldest promise of government ruled by the people is less an outcome than an ongoing question—one that continues to be answered by those who write the rules of America’s playbook, from Congress to the White House and beyond.
